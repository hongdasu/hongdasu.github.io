Design of a Hydrogel-based Moisture-Enabled Electric Generator (MEG)

Jul. 2026 - Present

with Prof. Chang Liu (Department of Mechanical Engineering, NUS, Singapore)

  • Preparing electrodes via electrochemical deposition and characterizing them.
  • Fabricating the device and testing its performance.

A Moisture-Enabled Electric Generator (MEG) is an innovative low-grade energy-harvesting device that converts the ubiquitous energy of atmospheric humidity directly into sustainable electrical power. By operating independently of sunlight or wind, MEGs offer a promising, continuous power solution for decentralized micro-electronics, such as wearable sensors and IoT devices.

The fundamental working principle of an MEG relies on moisture-induced ion migration. The device utilizes hydrophilic active materials that are rich in ionizable functional groups (e.g., carboxyl or sulfonic acid groups) to adsorb water molecules from the ambient environment. This hydration triggers the dissociation of the surface functional groups, releasing free, mobile charge carriers, most commonly protons (H+).

Electricity generation is driven by the establishment of a spatial gradient. Whether created by an engineered functional group gradient within the material or an asymmetric hydration gradient caused by directional moisture exposure, this imbalance forces the mobile ions to diffuse directionally from high to low concentration areas. Because the oppositely charged functional groups remain immobile on the material's backbone, this directional ionic flux effectively separates charges. This process builds up an electrical potential difference (voltage) between the electrodes, driving a continuous electric current through the external circuit.

Recommended Reviews:

  • Hydrovoltaic Electricity Generator with Hygroscopic Materials (Adv. Mater.)
  • Ion-Valence Engineering in Moisture-Enabled Electric Generator (Adv. Funct. Mater.)
  • Emerging bioinspired hydrovoltaic electricity generators (Energy Environ. Sci.)

Conventional MEGs typically rely on ion diffusion to establish an electrical double layer (EDL) at the electrode interface to generate a potential difference. However, both the output voltage magnitude and working duration remain severely constrained. While introducing active metal electrodes to engage in surface redox reactions can significantly boost power output, such designs suffer from limited operational lifespan and poor cyclability. To overcome these bottlenecks, this project investigates multivalent transition metal electrode materials (with a customized hydrogel system facilitating directional H+ transport) that facilitate the transfer of accumulated interfacial charges into external electrical output, enabling a rechargeable and long-lasting MEG design. Within this project, my work focuses on exploring high-surface-area Ni@Co electrodeposited electrodes and evaluating their performance.

Design of a Thermal Metastructure for Thermal Camouflage

Oct. 2025 - Present

with Prof. Liqun He (School of Engineering Science, USTC, China)

  • Proposing a method for designing thermal metamaterials to reshape heat sources during transient heat conduction.
  • Developing 2D and 3D models with script-assisted COMSOL Multiphysics.
  • Conducting FEA simulations.

Thermal metamaterials (also referred to as thermal metastructures) are artificial composite architectures designed to control and manipulate heat transfer—encompassing conduction, convection, and radiation—in manners previously considered impossible with natural media. While natural materials possess intrinsic and largely invariable thermal properties, thermal metamaterials derive their extraordinary behaviors primarily from their engineered spatial configurations, inhomogeneous geometric layouts, and structural anisotropy.

Functionally, these artificial structures have revolutionized active heat management and thermal signal processing. Prominent device archetypes include thermal cloaks, which bypass heat around an internal protected zone without disturbing the external isotherms; thermal concentrators, which focus heat flux into designated regions for efficient thermal energy harvesting; and thermal rotators or inverters, which twist or redirect heat currents. Furthermore, active and non-linear implementations enable macroscopic thermal diodes, zero-differential devices, and advanced thermal camouflage or illusion platforms that disguise actual thermal signatures with engineered exterior profiles.

The design methodologies for thermal metamaterials span both macroscopic phenomenological theory and microscopic carrier transport. At the macroscale, foundational methods rely on transformation thermotics, exploiting the coordinate form-invariance of conduction and diffusion equations to calculate required conductivity tensors. To address the resulting singularity and extreme anisotropy, the scattering cancellation technique offers simplified multilayered and bilayer designs with natural isotropic materials, while computational topology optimization facilitates free-form structural synthesis. Concurrently, at the micro- and nanoscale, phononic engineering and thermal photonics allow direct tailoring of phonon dispersion, localized resonances, and radiative emissivity spectra.

Recommended Reviews:

  • Transforming heat transfer with thermal metamaterials and devices (Nat. Rev. Mater.)
  • Thermal Metamaterial: Fundamental, Application, and Outlook (iScience)
  • Transformation Laplacian metamaterials: recent advances in manipulating thermal and dc fields (J. Opt.)

Traditional thermal metamaterial designs are predominantly constrained to steady-state fields, rendering them inadequate for highly dynamic and transient practical scenarios. In military applications, for instance, conventional metamaterials and thermal radiation coatings can successfully disguise the infrared (IR) signature of a real heat source into arbitrary shapes—but only under steady-state conditions. In real-world operations, the transition from heat source activation to the establishment of a steady thermal state can take hours. During this prolonged transient period, the true IR signature remains exposed or severely distorted, significantly undermining the tactical value of thermal camouflage. Similarly, in civilian sectors, while recent studies have leveraged the IR characteristics of structured materials to create infrared displays, these displays remain inherently static, fundamentally differing from digitally controlled, dynamic electronic screens.

To address these limitations, the core objective of this project is to achieve high-degree-of-freedom, transient, and continuous mapping between a true heat source and a camouflaged source within a time-dependent thermal diffusion field. Specifically, the region between the active heat source and the outer isothermal boundary is systematically stratified, with distinct coordinate transformations applied to each layer. By employing spatial discretization alongside variable-thickness modulation techniques, we translated these theoretical mappings into 3D-machinable structural models, which were subsequently validated through rigorous thermal experiments.

The transient thermal camouflage regulation mechanism proposed in this work holds significant promise for applications in dynamic thermal management and thermal encryption. Notably, this research has been recognized and supported by a grant from the NSFC Youth Student Basic Research Project.

Design of Electrothermal Metamaterials for Decoupling Potential and Flux Control

May. 2025 - Dec. 2025

with Prof. Liqun He (School of Engineering Science, USTC) and Prof. Gang Zhao (School of Information Science and Technology, USTC)

  • Assisting in developing 2D and 3D metamaterials models.
  • Conducting FEA simulations with COMSOL.
  • Experimentally verifying the metamaterials’ performance under thermal and electric fields.

Thermal metamaterials (also referred to as thermal metastructures) are artificial composite architectures designed to control and manipulate heat transfer—encompassing conduction, convection, and radiation—in manners previously considered impossible with natural media. While natural materials possess intrinsic and largely invariable thermal properties, thermal metamaterials derive their extraordinary behaviors primarily from their engineered spatial configurations, inhomogeneous geometric layouts, and structural anisotropy.

Recommended Reviews:

  • Transforming heat transfer with thermal metamaterials and devices (Nat. Rev. Mater.)
  • Thermal Metamaterial: Fundamental, Application, and Outlook (iScience)
  • Transformation Laplacian metamaterials: recent advances in manipulating thermal and dc fields (J. Opt.)

Multifunctional metamaterials have emerged as powerful platforms for manipulating physical fields through deliberately engineered structures, with applications ranging from thermal camouflage and energy transport to integrated electronics. However, in conventional electro-thermal metamaterials, regulating the voltage field is generally accompanied by corresponding changes in the current distribution, while manipulating temperature inevitably affects heat flux. As a result, existing designs are typically limited to one or two functionalities, making it difficult to independently control voltage and current, temperature and heat flux within a single metamaterial. This limitation restricts the development of multifunctional systems for applications such as adaptive camouflage, thermal management, energy routing, and reconfigurable electronic devices.

To address this challenge, this project developed a programmable electrothermal quad-functional metamaterial (ETQFM) based on a field-line-guided coordinate transformation strategy. The key idea is to sequentially perform two coordinate transformations: the first transformation is applied along current or heat-flux streamlines to prescribe the transport pathways and realize flux concentration, dispersion, or rotation; the second transformation is performed along equipotential or isothermal lines to independently modify the corresponding potential or temperature distributions. The resulting transformations generate spatially varying anisotropic electrical and thermal conductivity tensors, allowing potential and flux to be manipulated independently within the same structure.

To translate the theoretically required anisotropic tensors into manufacturable structures, the design domain is discretized into finite subunits. Within each subunit, the target anisotropy is approximated using rotated layers with different effective conductivities. Importantly, we employ a monolithic, interfacial-thermal-resistance-free thickness-engineering strategy, in which the anisotropic responses are realized within a single aluminum-alloy structure rather than through conventional dissimilar-material interfaces. The resulting architectures are fabricated by high-precision laser-sintering-based 3D printing and experimentally validated through both infrared thermal imaging and high-resolution electrical measurements.

Research Training in Framework Nucleic Acids (FNAs)

Feb. 2025 - May. 2025

with Prof. Tao Li (School of Chemistry and Materials Science, USTC)

  • Studying the fundamental concepts and recent advances in FNAs-based macromolecular nanostructure design through literature reviews.

During my one-semester research training in Prof. Tao Li's group, I engaged in extensive literature reviews and group discussions, which sparked a profound interest in Framework Nucleic Acids (FNAs). This field focuses on the design of nucleic acid sequences, leveraging programmable hydrogen bonding to self-assemble functional micro- and nanostructures.

By integrating dynamic structural transformations—such as toehold-mediated strand displacement, i-motifs, and G-quadruplexes—these micro-architectures can respond to specific environmental stimuli, including target RNAs, Mg2+ ions, pH variations, and ATP. This responsiveness enables the construction of complex molecular logic gates, showing promise in biomedical applications like targeted drug delivery and the highly sensitive detection of low-abundance biomarkers. Moreover, introducing specific chemical modifications to the nucleic acid strands can endow these programmable assemblies with unique catalytic capabilities.

From my perspective, highly customizable polymers like nucleic acids possess tremendous, untapped potential for bridging micro-scale molecular manipulation with macro-scale material properties. I believe that the insights I gained from this training will profoundly inspire and broaden my future interdisciplinary research horizons.
